# Threadwell

### Beautiful embroidery, made wonderfully simple.

**What this is (in plain English):** Threadwell is a simple app that helps someone put a design onto fabric with a Baby Lock Destiny sewing machine, without needing to learn complicated software. It is built to feel as easy as using an iPhone: big pictures to tap, big letters and numbers, and only a few choices on each screen. It replaces the scary, crowded professional software (Floriani Total Control U) with a calm, friendly helper that does the hard thinking for you.

---

## 1. The one-minute version

Your mom owns a wonderful embroidery machine, the **Baby Lock Destiny**. It can stitch almost anything. But the software people use to prepare designs, **Floriani Total Control U (FTC-U)**, is a professional tool with twelve chapters of dense menus, hundreds of settings, and toolbars that look like an airplane cockpit. For a 60-70 year old who just wants to put a flower on a towel, it is overwhelming.

**Threadwell throws all of that away.** Instead of a cockpit, it gives her a wall of big design pictures. She taps the one she wants. She taps what she is stitching it on (a towel, a t-shirt, a baby onesie). She picks a size and a color look. Then she taps one big green button, and the app gets her USB stick ready to carry to the machine. That is the whole thing: **three real decisions, big and clear, with the app quietly handling all the technical parts she should never have to think about.**

Behind that simple surface, Threadwell does a surprising amount of clever work: it stops the single most common mistake (a design too big for the hoop) before it can happen, it rescues designs that arrive with no colors, it makes the USB stick correctly so the machine never says "no design found," and it can even walk her through fixing thread breaks or lining up big designs. The philosophy is simple: **make it feel like an iPhone, and make it so she truly cannot break anything.**

---

## 2. Who this is for & the promise

**Who this is for:** A hobbyist, roughly 60 to 70 years old, who owns a Baby Lock Destiny (or Destiny II). She is comfortable and confident with her iPhone 14. She loves the idea of embroidery. But the professional software makes her feel stupid, and she is afraid of ruining an expensive shirt. She does not want to become a "digitizer" (a person who designs stitches). She just wants to put a nice design on a piece of fabric and have it come out well.

**The promise — three simple ideas:**

**1. As easy as an iPhone.** If she can send a photo to her grandkids, she can use this. We copy the patterns Apple already taught her: tap the thing itself, a Back button always in the same corner, standard gestures, immediate feedback on every tap. Nothing new to learn.

**2. Big tiles, big letters, big numbers.** Every important control is a large tappable tile with a real picture and a word under it. Body text is large. Sizes and counts are shown in huge numbers. No tiny gray text, no dense menus, no dropdown lists to hunt through.

> **A quick word on why "big" matters.** As we age, our eyes and fingertips change. This is not a preference, it is physical. Fingertip touch sensitivity roughly doubles in "fuzziness" from young adulthood to the late sixties, so buttons need to be bigger and spaced further apart. The eye's lens stiffens and yellows, which makes small text and pale colors genuinely hard to read. Threadwell is designed around these real changes, not around what looks trendy.

**3. You truly cannot break it.** Every action can be undone. Her real design file is never touched; edits always happen on a copy. Dangerous choices are blocked with a plain reason instead of failing silently. There is a calm "Help Me" button always within reach. She can poke at anything without fear.

## 3. How it works — the 3 taps

The heart of Threadwell is one gentle, linear path. Each screen asks **one thing**, shows only a **few big choices**, and never rushes her. Nothing auto-advances. She moves at her own pace, and she can always go Back.

Here is the core workflow, screen by screen.

### Step 1 — Pick a design
She sees a **wall of large design photos**, two across, newest first, on a clean white background. At the very top is a fat search box that says **"What are you looking for?"** with a microphone icon so she can just speak ("Christmas snowman"). Under it, three friendly category tiles: **Flowers, Holidays, Words.** There are **no filenames anywhere** — just pictures, the way the Photos app shows her grandkids. Every design already appears in sensible colors.

> **One action:** Tap the design photo she wants.

### Step 2 — What are you stitching it on?
A big heading: **"What are you putting it on?"** Below it, six large photo tiles she recognizes instantly: **Towel, T-shirt front, Baby onesie, Tote bag, Hat, Something else.** This single answer quietly sets a safe size, the right hoop, and the right backing behind the scenes — so she never has to touch size numbers or learn hoop names.

> **One action:** Tap the item (for example, Towel).

### Step 3 — Choose the size
The design appears **large, sitting on a real photo of a towel**, so the scale looks real. Three plain tiles: **Small, Medium, Large.** The chosen size shows in **huge numbers, in both inches and centimeters** (for example, "5 in / 13 cm"). A green check line reads **"Fits your hoop."** If a size would be too big, that tile turns amber, says **"Bigger than your hoop can hold,"** and cannot be tapped. **This blocks the single most common beginner mistake before it can happen.** Medium is pre-selected as a safe default, so she can simply move on.

> **One action:** Tap Small, Medium, or Large.

### Step 4 — Choose the colors
A full-screen preview of the design on her fabric. Below it, four big swatch tiles: **Original, Autumn, Ocean, Black & White.** She picks **a look, not a thread number.** A small padlock chip reassures her: **"Original saved and safe — this is a copy."** If the design arrived with no colors (common with certain files), a green banner says **"This design had no colors, so I set them for you — you can change them."** No scary rainbow mess, ever.

> **One action:** Tap a color look (or leave it on Original and move on).

### Step 5 — Get your USB stick ready
A calm confirm screen shows the finished design, its size in huge numbers, and one plain line: **"For your Baby Lock Destiny — Towel — 5 in — fits your hoop."** One big green button: **"MAKE MY STICK."** She plugs her USB stick into the phone (or does this step on the computer) and taps. A short checklist animates with green checks she can read across the room: **"Right format ✓  Unzipped ✓  In the right place ✓  Fits your hoop ✓."** Then a friendly card: **"Ready! Take this stick to your Destiny,"** with a photo of the stick going into the machine.

> **One action:** Tap the one big green button.

## 4. The home screen

The home screen has **just three big, stacked tiles** — because research on older users found that about three top-level choices is the sweet spot. Each tile is a full-width rectangle with a large icon, a plain-language label, and a one-line description.

| Tile | Icon | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Stitch a Design** | 🧵 | The main path. Opens the picture-wall of designs to pick one and send it to the machine. Biggest tile, top of screen. |
| **My Designs** | 🖼️ | A wall of design photos she already owns (newest first), so she can re-stitch something she made before. |
| **Help Me** | 🆘 | A big, calm help screen: fix thread breaks, "machine shows no designs," hooping steps, or call a helper. This is also the panic button. |

That is the entire home screen. No dashboard, no numbers to interpret, no clutter.

## 5. Design system (the big-and-clear rules)

These are the concrete rules that make Threadwell readable and tappable for aging eyes and hands. They come straight from accessibility research (Apple's guidelines, the WCAG accessibility standard, and studies on older adults). Every number here is a floor to beat, not a target to just barely meet.

### Sizes and spacing

| Thing | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Main tiles (home) | Full-width, ~340 × 140 px, 20 px gap | Readable and tappable from arm's length |
| Design-pick tiles | ~165 × 165 px, two across, 16 px gaps | Big enough to recognize the picture instantly |
| Size-preset tiles | ~150 × 150 px, three across | One clear row of choices |
| **Minimum touch target** | **60 × 60 px** for all primary controls (~15 mm) | Well above the 44 px floor; older adults prefer ~20 mm buttons |
| Space between tappable things | **16 px minimum** empty space | A slightly-off tap lands on nothing, not the wrong button |
| Page side margins | 24 px left and right | Comfortable, uncrowded |
| Corner radius | 16 px on tiles and buttons | Soft, familiar, iOS-like |
| Primary button height | 64 px, full width | Impossible to miss |

### Text sizes

| Text | Size | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Body text | **20 pt** regular-weight sans-serif | Never below 16 pt, never a thin weight, never all-caps |
| Button labels | **22 pt bold** | |
| Headings | **34 pt bold** | |
| Huge numbers (sizes, counts, steps) | **40 pt bold** | Sizes and step counts should read across the room |

All text sizes scale with the iPhone's own **system text-size setting** (called Dynamic Type). If she has already made her phone's text bigger, Threadwell inherits that automatically, and the layout reflows without clipping.

### Colors and contrast

| Purpose | Color | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Text | #1A1A1A near-black on #FFFFFF white | ~16:1 contrast — far exceeds the strictest accessibility level (AAA needs 7:1) |
| Primary "go" action | #0B7A3B green with white text | Used **only** for the safe/go action, always paired with a word |
| Warning | #9A5B00 amber text/border on pale background | Always with an icon **and** plain words — never amber alone |
| Danger (delete) | #B00020 red | Reserved for destructive actions, kept physically far from the green button |
| Disabled | #C9C9C9 gray with a lock icon and a reason | So "disabled" never looks like the app is broken |

**Two rules that matter enormously for aging vision:**

- **Never color alone.** Every status uses color **plus** an icon **plus** a word. A green check that says "Fits your hoop." An amber triangle that says "Too big for your hoop." Someone who cannot tell the colors apart still gets the message.
- **No pure blue for small important text.** The aging eye's lens filters out blue light, so pure blue on small text reads faintly. We keep blues dark and bold, and never carry meaning by "blue versus green" or "blue versus purple."

## 6. The real problems in embroidery — and our over-engineered fixes

This is the heart of the design. Embroidery has a lot of hidden traps, and beginners usually discover them **after** they have ruined a garment. Threadwell's job is to catch each trap **before** it costs her fabric, thread, and an afternoon.

Below, the real headaches are grouped into four families. For each headache, we describe it in plain English, then give **one or more solutions** — often several, because a good app can attack the same problem from different angles. Each solution shows its **tagline**, **what mom actually sees**, and a **feasibility tag**:

- **Buildable now** — we could ship this with today's technology.
- **Moderate** — real work, but clearly doable.
- **Hard R&D** — a genuine research problem; promise carefully.

---

### FAMILY A — Files, formats, finding & organizing designs

#### Headache A1: The format zoo — "which file does my machine even need?"
There are a dozen-plus file types (.pes, .dst, .jef, .vp3, .exp, and more) and no single universal one. A download often contains a pile of them, and a beginner has no idea which one to copy over. Pick wrong and the machine either can't see the file or reads it badly.

**Solution: My One File** — *"You have one machine. So you only ever need one file."*
The app learns once that she owns a Baby Lock Destiny. From then on, the whole idea of "formats" **disappears from her view.** Every design is silently prepared as the correct file type (PES) at a version her machine can read.
- **What mom sees:** Setup shows one question with two big tiles and a photo of each machine: "Which sewing machine do you have?" She taps her Destiny. Done forever. After that, every design screen just has one big green button: "MAKE FOR MY DESTINY." No format words, ever.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

**Solution: Point-and-Know** — *"Not sure which file? Show me. I'll pick the right one."*
For the moment she is staring at a messy download, she shares the folder into the app, and it identifies the single file her Destiny needs, dims the rest, and hands her the keeper.
- **What mom sees:** One big card with a picture of the design and the words "This is the one your Destiny needs." Everything else is collapsed under "Files you can ignore (11)." One button: "USE THIS ONE."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.** (An extra "point your camera at the laptop screen and I'll circle the right file" mode is possible but harder.)

**Solution: Badge Scan Setup** — *"Scan your machine once. Never think about compatibility again."*
Instead of even the two-tile choice, she photographs the machine's silver name badge and the app recognizes "Destiny II" and configures everything, including which hoops she likely owns.
- **What mom sees:** A camera viewfinder: "Point at the silver name badge." A chime, then "Baby Lock Destiny II — got it!" with a checkmark.
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

#### Headache A2: Converting between formats quietly ruins the design
Free converters make it look like any format cleanly becomes any other. It doesn't. Converting to the "universal" DST format **drops all the colors.** Converting a finished stitch file never restores the editable design underneath. You get a file that opens but stitches wrong — and you only find out in the fabric.

**Solution: The Original Vault** — *"We never touch your real design. Ever."*
Every design she brings in is stored untouched as a permanent master. Anything the app makes (a resized copy, a recolored version) is a disposable child copy. Because the master is never overwritten, she can always go back to the pristine original.
- **What mom sees:** A small padlock chip: "Original saved and safe." If she changes anything: "This is a copy. Your original is untouched." Always one button: "Go back to the original."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.** (An extra visual "family tree" of every copy she's made is a lovely optional flourish.)

**Solution: Damage Preview** — *"See exactly what a conversion will ruin — before it happens."*
When a change would actually lose something, the app simulates the outcome and shows a plain before/after.
- **What mom sees:** A split screen: left "Now" (correct colors), right "After" ("Colors would be lost — the machine would ask you to pick every thread by hand"). Two well-separated buttons: "KEEP IT SAFE" (green) and "Do it anyway."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.** (An extra "this mistake would waste about 40 minutes and 3 bobbins" estimate makes the warning concrete.)

**Solution: Refuse to Ruin** — *"The app simply won't let you stitch something broken."*
For the most dangerous conversions, the app blocks the bad path and quietly routes around it — recovering colors before it lets her proceed, and never exporting a file version her machine can't open.
- **What mom sees:** No scary failure. Instead: "I fixed this for you," and one sentence explaining what it did. One button: "Looks good."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

#### Headache A3: DST files carry no colors, so you re-guess the palette every time
The DST format only stores stitch moves and "stop and change thread here" — not the actual colors. So a DST opens as a random rainbow and you must reassign every thread by hand.

**Solution: Color Rescue** — *"Snap the designer's color sheet — we'll paint the design for you."*
She photographs the seller's color-list picture, and the app reads the colors and stitch order and applies them automatically, mapping each to a real Baby Lock thread.
- **What mom sees:** A DST opens as nonsense rainbow. A banner: "This design came with no colors. Let's fix it." Big button: "Take a photo of the color list." She snaps the paper. The design redraws correctly. "Done — 6 colors set."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Try-On Colors** — *"Pick a look, not a thread number."*
When there is no color sheet, she picks from ready-made "colorways" previewed on her actual fabric.
- **What mom sees:** Four big swatched tiles: "Autumn," "Ocean," "Berry," "Black & White." She taps one and the design instantly repaints. She can set her fabric color first so the preview looks real.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

**Solution: Stop-by-Stop Guided Coloring** — *"One color at a time. Point at your spool, we do the rest."*
A fail-safe fallback that shows one color block at a time so she is never overwhelmed by all eight at once.
- **What mom sees:** "COLOR 1 OF 8." Only that part of the design glows; the rest is grayed. She taps "PICK A COLOR" or "SNAP MY SPOOL" (the camera reads the thread color). Progress dots show how far along she is. She can go Back anytime.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

#### Headache A4: You can't just resize a design like a photo
People expect to stretch a design to fit. But a stitch file has fixed stitches baked in. Stretch it too far and scaling up leaves gaps with fabric showing through; scaling down piles stitches into a hard knot that breaks needles.

**Solution: Safe-Size Dial** — *"A size dial that turns red when you're about to break the design."*
As she changes size, the app shows green while she's in the safe range and clearly warns the moment she pushes too far.
- **What mom sees:** A big preview and a large slider (or plus/minus tiles), with the size in huge inches-and-centimeters. The track is green in the safe zone; drag too far and it turns amber: "Bigger than this can leave gaps." A "Best fit for my hoop" shortcut sits underneath.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.** (An optional live "density heatmap" shows the knot forming before it exists.)

**Solution: True Resize** — *"Actually re-stitch the design at the new size — not just stretch it."*
The heavy hitter: instead of stretching fixed stitches, the app rebuilds the stitches at the new size the way a professional would, so she can go well beyond the safe range.
- **What mom sees:** She types "I need this 8 inches wide." A short friendly wait — "Re-stitching your design at 8 inches..." — then a fresh preview. She never learns the word "density."
- **Feasibility: Hard R&D.** (Doing this reliably from any file is genuinely difficult; it falls back to the Safe-Size Dial when it can't.)

**Solution: Fit My Thing** — *"Tell me what you're stitching on. I'll size it to fit."*
Reframes resizing around the real goal — fitting an actual item — instead of abstract percentages.
- **What mom sees:** Big tiles: "Towel," "T-shirt front," "Baby onesie," "Tote bag," "Hat." She taps "Towel," then picks Small/Medium/Large shown at real scale on a towel photo. The app sizes it safely and confirms it fits her hoop.
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

#### Headache A5: Drowning in thousands of design files you can't search or preview
Collections balloon into thousands of files with cryptic names, spread across USB sticks and folders. Windows won't even show thumbnails for these files, so a folder is a wall of identical icons. People re-buy designs they already own because they can't find them.

**Solution: My Designs, Like Photos** — *"A wall of pictures, not a wall of filenames."*
Turns the scattered mess into one visual library that looks and behaves like the iPhone Photos app — every design a real thumbnail, duplicates quietly merged.
- **What mom sees:** A grid of design pictures, big and tappable, newest first. Pinch to enlarge. No filenames. Identical designs merge with a small "2 copies" note. Big tabs: "All," "Favorites," "Recently used."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

**Solution: Just Ask For It** — *"Say 'the blue flower for towels' and it appears."*
An AI layer tags every design so she can search by plain description, the way she searches photos of her grandkids.
- **What mom sees:** A big search box: "What are you looking for?" She types or speaks "Christmas snowman" and matching designs appear as pictures. Friendly auto-made tiles: "Flowers," "Holidays," "Kids," "Words."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Find It By Photo** — *"Take a picture of the finished piece — get the file back."*
For the classic "I stitched this once and can't find the file," she photographs the finished item and the app finds the matching design.
- **What mom sees:** A "Find by photo" button. She snaps the embroidered towel. "Found it!" with the exact design and a "Make it again" button.
- **Feasibility: Hard R&D.** (Matching a real stitched object back to a file is a hard vision problem.)

#### Headache A6: Licensing roulette — "am I even allowed to sell this?"
Every designer writes their own license. Some allow 50 finished items, some 200, some ban selling online. Buyers think they bought the design, but they only licensed a use. It is genuinely hard to know what you are allowed to make and sell.

**Solution: License Locker** — *"Every design carries its own permission slip — in plain English."*
When she buys a design, the app staples the receipt and license to it permanently and translates the legalese into one clear line.
- **What mom sees:** On any design, a tab: "What am I allowed to do?" It shows: "You may make and sell up to 200 finished items. You may NOT share or resell the file." Plus "Bought from [shop], March 2026 — receipt saved."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Can I Sell This?** — *"A traffic light for the one question that actually scares her."*
Distills the mess to the one decision most people face: is it OK to sell what I made?
- **What mom sees:** A big status chip with an icon **and** words: green check "YES — you can sell this," yellow dot "CHECK — limited, tap to see," red slash "NO — personal use only." Never color alone.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

**Solution: Item Tally** — *"Count the shirts you've made against the number you're allowed."*
For licenses that cap the number of items, the app keeps the running count.
- **What mom sees:** A big counter: "37 of 200 made," and a large "+1 I made another" button. Nearing the limit, it turns amber: "190 of 200 — getting close."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

#### Headache A7: USB "gotchas" that make the machine say "no design found"
You copy a design to a USB stick, plug it in, and the machine shows nothing. It wants a specific format (FAT32), files loose in the main folder (not inside a zip or a subfolder), and often a small older drive. None of this is obvious, and it fails silently.

**Solution: USB Butler** — *"One tap makes a machine-ready stick — no more 'no design found.'"*
Automates every gotcha at once: correct format, files unzipped, dropped in the right place with short names, sized to fit.
- **What mom sees:** She plugs in the stick and taps "Get my stick ready." A checklist animates with green checks: "Right format ✓  Unzipped ✓  In the right place ✓  Fits your hoop ✓." Then "Ready! Plug this into your Destiny." If a step can't be done on the phone, it says exactly what to do next.
- **Feasibility: Moderate.** (An optional Threadwell-branded pre-formatted USB stick makes the whole path foolproof.)

**Solution: Why-Won't-It-Read Helper** — *"The machine says nothing. This app tells you exactly which rule you broke."*
A diagnostic for the dead-end moment at the machine.
- **What mom sees:** Big tiles: "Machine shows no designs," "Machine shows an error," "Some designs missing." She taps one; it inspects her stick or asks one big yes/no at a time, ending on one clear instruction: "The stick is the wrong format — here's how to fix it."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

#### Headache A8: Buying designs is quality roulette
A flat listing photo tells you nothing about how well a design is actually made. A cheap, auto-traced file looks identical to a well-made one — until it puckers, breaks thread, or leaves gaps when you stitch it.

**Solution: Stitch X-Ray** — *"Look inside a design before you buy it — not after you've wasted a shirt."*
Inspects the actual stitch file and grades the digitizing quality a picture hides.
- **What mom sees:** A "Check quality" button. Result is one big grade with words and an icon: "Good digitizing — should stitch clean" or "Risky — lots of jumps, may break thread," with a few plain bullets.
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Trusted Shops** — *"Buy from sellers other Destiny owners actually stitched — and loved."*
Trust earned from real stitched results by other users on the same machine.
- **What mom sees:** Each design shows a big trust badge with words: "Loved by 1,200 Destiny owners" or "New seller — not yet proven." Reviews come from people who actually stitched it, each with a real finished photo.
- **Feasibility: Hard R&D.** (Building a trustworthy, model-specific reputation graph plus copy-detection is a big undertaking.)

### FAMILY B — Thread, color & lettering (names and monograms)

#### Headache B1: Downloaded designs open with no colors — guessing the whole stop sequence
A common problem (especially with DST files): the design opens as a rainbow of random colors, and you have to guess what each of the 8 stops should be, and in what order.

**Solution: Color Detective (Fingerprint Match)** — *"That mystery file? We already know its colors."*
The app computes a "fingerprint" of the stitch data and looks it up in a shared library. If it matches a known design, it restores the exact colors automatically.
- **What mom sees:** She adds a design; a preview appears in real colors with a green banner: "We found the colors for this design." One button: "USE THESE COLORS."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Preview-to-Palette (Photo of the Picture)** — *"Show us the picture from the shop — we'll fill in the thread order."*
She gives the app the seller's preview image; AI matches the colored regions to the design's color stops in the right order.
- **What mom sees:** "I HAVE A PICTURE OF IT." She picks or snaps the image. The colors appear as a numbered stack of fat swatches with real thread names: "1 Navy, 2 Red, 3 White…" One button: "LOOKS RIGHT."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

**Solution: Stop-by-Stop Guided Coloring** — *(also serves Family A)* — *"One color at a time. Point at your spool, we do the rest."*
The never-stuck fallback: one color stop per screen, name it by tapping or by photographing the spool. Each finished design is saved into her personal "Embroidery Journal" so the colors are remembered next time.
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.**

#### Headache B2: Small letters and names turn into unreadable mush
Text under about 4-5 mm tall blobs together, thin strokes vanish, and the thread keeps breaking. A fancy script at that size is hopeless — and you usually find out after ruining a garment.

**Solution: Will-It-Stitch Meter** — *"A green light before you sew, not a ruined shirt after."*
As she sizes a name, the app checks it against known limits and shows a simple traffic light before anything reaches the machine.
- **What mom sees:** She types the name and drags one "BIGGER / SMALLER" control. A big readout shows the height: "4 mm." A single light with words: GREEN "This will stitch clean," YELLOW "Getting risky," RED "Too small, letters will blob." When green: "STITCH THIS."
- **Feasibility: Buildable now.** (An optional "1:1 on your phone" mode shows the name at exact real size over the camera.)

**Solution: Tiny-Text Auto-Rescue** — *"Too small? Tap once — we make it stitchable."*
One button re-engineers the text for small-scale success: swaps a fussy script for a clean font, lightens the stitches, adds proper support, and recommends the thread and needle.
- **What mom sees:** On a yellow/red verdict, a "FIX IT FOR ME" button. It shows a clean before/after: "We switched to an easy-to-read font and made the stitches lighter so the thread won't break." Buttons: "USE THE FIX" or "KEEP MINE," plus a gentle note: "Use 60-weight thread and a fine needle."
- **Feasibility: Moderate.**

## 7. Made for your Baby Lock Destiny

Threadwell is built specifically around **your** machine — the **Baby Lock Destiny** (model BLDY, launched around 2013) or the **Destiny II** (BLDY2, around 2016-2017). These are top-of-the-line Brother-built machines. (The Destiny is the twin of Brother's "Dream Machine"; the Destiny II is the twin of the "Dream Machine 2." Feet and hoops swap between the siblings.)

Knowing the exact machine lets us do the hard technical thinking so she never has to. Here are the machine facts that shape the app.

### What file the machine wants

| Format | What it is | How Threadwell treats it |
|---|---|---|
| **PES** | The machine's native, preferred file. Carries the thread colors. | **This is what we always make.** It shows the right color names on the machine. |
| **PEC** | The stitch block that lives inside a PES. | Handled automatically inside PES. |
| **DST** | A common format that stitches fine but carries **no colors.** | We can read it, but we recover or set colors first so she isn't left guessing. |
| **PHC** | The machine's own private save format. | We do **not** try to author this — it's an output format, not a good starting point. |

> **Why PES:** It is native to the Destiny and it carries the thread colors, so the machine shows the correct color names and stitch order. DST would force her to assign every color by hand at the machine. (Confidence: **high** — corroborated by Baby Lock's own spec listing of ".pes, .phc, .pec, .dst" and multiple format guides.)

### How designs get to the machine

**There is no wireless.** Neither the Destiny nor the Destiny II has built-in Wi-Fi. An app can **never** push a design to the machine over the air. Every route ends in a **physical copy**:

- **USB flash drive** into one of the machine's two USB ports (the drive should be **FAT32** formatted).
- **SD card** into the machine's card slot.
- **USB-B cable** to a Windows PC (the machine shows up as a removable drive).

> Threadwell's whole job at the end is to make that physical copy **foolproof** — right format, unzipped, files loose in the main folder, short filenames, sized to fit. (Confidence: **high** that there is no wireless. A stray web page titled "How to Connect Wi-Fi on Baby Lock Destiny II" exists, but those page titles are auto-generated and unreliable, and it almost certainly confuses the newer wireless Solaris machines. We did not treat it as real.)

### Hoop sizes (the frames that hold the fabric)

| Hoop | Size | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 4" × 4" | 100 × 100 mm | Small designs |
| 5" × 7" | 130 × 180 mm | Very common |
| 6" × 10" | 150 × 250 mm | Fits both models |
| 8" × 12" | 200 × 300 mm | Large |
| **9.5" × 14"** | **240 × 360 mm** | **The largest — the full field, in one pass** |
| 9.5" × 9.5" | 240 × 240 mm | Quilt hoop (standard on Destiny II) |
| Scan frame | — | For scanning artwork into the machine |

> The **absolute maximum** design area is **240 × 360 mm (9.5" × 14")** — and it's a true single-pass field, so no re-hooping is needed even at that size. But every design must **also fit the specific hoop she's using** (often much smaller). **The "design too big for the hoop" rejection is the single most common user error** — which is exactly why Threadwell blocks it up front, in plain language, before the file ever leaves the phone. (Confidence: **high** on the max field; **medium** on the exact bundled hoop set, which varies by dealer and promotion.)

### Other machine facts we design around

- **Single needle.** The machine stitches one color at a time and **stops to prompt a manual thread change** at each color. So minimizing color changes and grouping colors sensibly is a real usability win — several Threadwell features do this automatically.
- **File version care.** A too-new PES version can fail to load on older Destiny firmware, so we target a broadly compatible version.
- **Modest file sizes.** Onboard memory is limited (single-digit megabytes), so we keep stitch counts reasonable.
- **Short filenames, shallow folders.** The firmware limits filename length and how deep folders can nest, so we keep names short and files loose.
- **Thread color mapping.** We map colors to a supported thread chart (color + brand index) so the machine shows correct color names instead of falling back to a default palette.

### What the machine can already do on its own
The Destiny has a big 10.1-inch color touchscreen and **IQ Designer** (Baby Lock's on-screen design tool, the same feature Brother calls "My Design Center"). It can scan artwork, edit, resize, letter, appliqué, and use its camera for placement. Threadwell doesn't replace all of that — it makes the **preparing and transferring** of designs simple enough that she actually enjoys getting to the fun part.

## 8. Grandma Mode — you truly cannot break it

Every one of these is a deliberate safety net, so she can explore with zero fear.

- **Undo everywhere.** Every screen keeps an "Undo" control, and "Go back to the original" is always one tap away. Her real design file is **never touched** — edits are always a copy.
- **Can't-break-it guardrails.** A size that won't fit the hoop is blocked with a plain reason ("Bigger than your hoop can hold") instead of failing silently at the machine. The #1 error is stopped up front.
- **Auto-repair, no scary errors.** A design with no colors is quietly fixed with sensible defaults and one plain sentence — never a rainbow mess or a technical error code.
- **A big confirm screen before anything final.** Making the USB stick shows a large plain-language summary ("For your Destiny — Towel — 5 in — fits your hoop") and one green button, so she always sees what will happen before it happens.
- **Destructive actions are separated and confirmed.** Delete is red, never next to the green "go" button, and always asks a plain yes/no with an Undo afterward.
- **No time limits, nothing auto-advances.** Screens wait for her. Work is preserved across steps and interruptions, so nothing is silently lost.
- **Read-aloud on every screen.** A "Read this to me" speaker button reads the title, the instructions, and the choices out loud.
- **A calm "Help Me" button, always reachable from Home.** One screen with "Machine shows no designs," "My thread keeps breaking," "Show me hooping" — asked one big yes/no at a time, ending in one clear instruction — plus a "Call my helper" button to reach a saved family contact.
- **USB Butler safety net.** One tap checks the format, unzips, places files correctly, and re-confirms the design fits the hoop, so "no design found" at the machine is prevented.
- **Recipe Card.** After a good run, "Save this to make again" lets her re-create the exact size, colors, and placement in one tap — recognition, not recall.
- **Consistent, always-visible Back and Home** in the same corner on every screen, so she can never feel stuck or lost.

**And the accessibility foundations underneath all of it:**

- Every primary control is **60 × 60 px or larger** with 16 px of empty space around it, so a slightly-off tap lands on nothing rather than the wrong button.
- **Near-black text on white** at ~16:1 contrast — far beyond the strictest standard. No gray-on-white, no thin fonts, no text over photos for important labels.
- Body text **20 pt**, buttons **22 pt bold**, numbers and headings **34-40 pt.**
- Full support for the **iPhone's system text-size setting**; the layout reflows and never clips when text is enlarged.
- **Never color alone** — every status is color **plus** an icon **plus** a word.
- **Aging-vision color care** — no meaning carried by blue-versus-green or blue-versus-purple; blues kept dark and bold.
- **Icon plus text on every control**, using familiar, photo-real icons rather than abstract glyphs.
- **One task per screen** with 3-6 large choices, following the iPhone patterns she already knows.
- **Immediate feedback on every tap** — a visual highlight plus a light buzz — so she always knows it registered.
- **Selection over typing** wherever possible (tap Small/Medium/Large, tap a color look), with numbers shown in both inches and centimeters.

## 9. How we'd actually build it

This section is the honest engineering picture — useful for the person actually building it.

### Platform: build it as a web app first

**Recommendation: a responsive web app (a "PWA," a website that installs like an app) as the product, plus an optional thin Windows helper.** One codebase then serves both the Windows PC (where the machine actually gets fed) and mom's iPhone 14 (where she browses and designs).

- **Why not an iPhone-only app?** The final file has to land on a physical USB stick, and the iPhone makes writing to a USB drive painfully hard for a casual user. And since the Destiny has no wireless, the iPhone could never send to it anyway. The iPhone's real role is "design and share to daughter," which a web app covers perfectly.
- **Why not only a Windows app?** A native Windows app is actually the **strongest** option for the one thing the web can't do well — detecting the inserted USB drive, checking it's FAT32, and copying the file with a correct short name. So we recommend that as an **optional companion "Transfer Helper," not the whole product.**
- **Practical shape:** Web app as the product; an optional thin Windows helper (built with a tool called Tauri, which reuses the same web screens) for one-click USB export. The same file engine sits behind both.

### The tech stack (what it's made of)

| Layer | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The screens (UI) | React or SvelteKit + TypeScript, installable as a PWA | One codebase for Windows browsers and the iPhone; type-safety keeps the geometry math sane |
| Design preview | HTML5 Canvas | Drawing a design is just drawing colored lines between stitch points; handles tens of thousands of stitches fine |
| File-format engine | **pyembroidery** (Python), server-side (or in-browser later) | The most reliable open reader/writer for PES/DST that preserves colors |
| Stitch-generation engine | **Ink/Stitch** algorithms (open source) | The only mature open-source code that actually *generates* good stitches (fills, satins, underlay, lettering) |
| Backend | Python (FastAPI) | Same language as the two hard libraries — no cross-language bridge |
| Storage | A database for details + object storage for the files | Designs are small; cheap and boring on purpose |
| Login & sharing | Passwordless email link or Google sign-in, plus shareable design links | Keeps a non-technical user out of the weeds (never any machine or payment credentials in-app) |
| Windows helper | Tauri (reuses the web screens) | Only the helper needs raw USB access; no need to rebuild the UI |
| Hosting | Static frontend on a CDN; Python service on a normal server | Fast app, real server for the heavy libraries |

### File formats — a mostly solved problem
Reading and writing files is the **easy** part thanks to **pyembroidery**: it reliably reads PES/PEC/DST and writes PES while keeping the thread colors. Plan: use pyembroidery for all import, preview, export, and hoop-bounds checking; target a broadly compatible PES version (verified on the real 2013 Destiny, since older firmware can reject newer files); write DST only as a fallback with a clear "this loses your colors" warning; and never try to author the machine's private PHC format.

### The honest truth about auto-digitizing
**Turning a photo into good stitches is the genuinely hard part** — it's what separates a weekend toy from the expensive professional tools. Be candid about it:

- **pyembroidery does NOT digitize.** It only moves files and coordinates around.
- **The only mature open-source stitch generator is Ink/Stitch**, and it works from **vector art (clean shapes)** that someone defines — not from a photo.
- So **"upload a picture, get beautiful embroidery" at commercial quality is a research problem, not a feature you can schedule.** Real stitch quality needs underlay, pull compensation, density control, stitch direction, and smart routing — the hard 20% that is 80% of the perceived quality.
- **One licensing note to decide early:** leaning on Ink/Stitch (the realistic path to any digitizing) likely means the whole product must also be open-source (GPL). That's a real business decision, not a detail.

### What's buildable right now vs. hard

**Buildable now (real, shippable value):**
- Import an existing PES/DST and show an accurate preview.
- **Validate the design against the chosen hoop and clearly warn "too big"** — this kills the #1 error and is trivial once you can read the design's size.
- Resize, rotate, mirror, reposition, and combine designs.
- Reorder and merge color blocks to cut manual thread changes.
- Lettering and monograms using ready-made embroidery fonts (placing existing letter-stitches, not inventing them).
- Export a machine-safe PES with a short filename, ready for a FAT32 stick.
- The mom-designs-on-iPhone, shares-to-daughter, daughter-exports-on-Windows flow.

**Genuinely hard (promise carefully):**
- True photo auto-digitizing at commercial quality.
- Correctly re-stitching a design at a new size (needs the digitizing engine, not just scaling).
- A convincing 3D thread preview.
- Pinning down the exact safe PES version for the old 2013 firmware (needs real-hardware testing).

## 12. Honest limitations & what I recommend building first

Let me be straight with you, because you're the one who has to build this.

**What's genuinely realistic:** The most valuable version of Threadwell — the one that removes 80% of your mom's daily frustration — is also the **most buildable.** It doesn't invent stitches. It takes designs she already owns, previews them clearly, **stops her from making the design too big for her hoop**, tidies the color order so she changes thread fewer times, and prepares a USB stick that the machine will actually read. Every one of those is doable with existing open-source tools (pyembroidery for files, a simple canvas for previews). None of it requires the hard AI.

**What's tempting but hard:** The dream of "point your camera at a photo and get perfect embroidery" is a genuine, decades-deep research problem. The big professional companies have poured years into it and their results **still** need human cleanup. If you promise that as a core feature, you'll be chasing it forever. Treat any photo-to-stitches feature as a bonus experiment with honest "you'll want to tidy this up" framing — never as the thing the app is *for*.

**The one unavoidable manual step:** Because the Destiny has no wireless, the app can never magically send a design to the machine. The last step is always walking a USB stick over. Don't fight that — **make it foolproof and celebratory** instead (the green-check "Ready! Take this stick to your Destiny" moment).

**My concrete recommendation for the first step:**

> **Build the MVP as a small Windows web app that does exactly four things:** (1) opens a design she already has, (2) shows a big, clear preview, (3) checks it against her chosen hoop and warns in plain language if it's too big, and (4) writes a correctly-formatted PES onto her USB stick with a short filename. Wrap it in the big-tile, big-text design system from Section 6. Sit with your mom and watch her use it. That single loop — open, check, save to stick — is the beating heart of the whole product, and it will already make her embroidery life dramatically easier. Everything else in this document is a layer you add **after** that core delights her.

Start there. Make the simple thing wonderful. Then grow.
